Host voice campaign review

A timely campaign about practical hospitality and a personal welcome. Prepared for Cara and Joe.

Campaign recommendation for Cara and Joe · October 30, 2026 · Draft for review

Decision

Test the opportunity, with a warmer and more practical message. The October policy announcement creates a timely reason for hosts to revisit their guest information. It does not establish demand for a new guestbook, or prove that introducing the host changes guest behavior.

Recommended lead: “Your welcome. Your words. The details guests need.”

Supporting line: “Meet your host. Find the coffee. Know trash day.”

Keep “control your own narrative” as the original strategic idea, rather than the public headline. Show what hosts can create: their introduction, useful house information, local favorites and a guestbook. Avoid implying control over guests, enforcement or booking-platform reviews. Host warmth is the emotional hook; practical answers are the product demonstration.

What actually changed

Airbnb announced its house-rules update on October 20, 2026. Its policy applies to reservations booked on or after November 20, 2026, rather than every stay taking place after that date. It consolidates seven standard rule categories and reasonable requests. Cleaner-type duties such as bed stripping, laundry, washing and putting away dishes, and off-site trash removal are generally excluded. The announcement still allows simpler requests such as putting dishes in the sink and sorting waste into designated indoor containers. Announcement

Rules and requests must be disclosed in the designated listing section before booking. A host cannot introduce new ones afterward through messages, signs or in person. Moving a request into a QR guide does not create an exemption. Informational wording can also imply an obligation: “pickup is Tuesday” must not be a concealed request to move trash cans. Exceptions depend on the listing and local circumstances; the guide cannot grant them. Policy

Guests are still expected to respect the home, avoid excessive mess, use designated waste receptacles and follow house rules. The change does not mean that respect is no longer expected. Guest ground rules

The product opportunity is therefore a personal, useful welcome alongside the disclosed booking terms. Avoid “they won't let you tell guests anything” or “say what you can't say on your listing”: both misstate the opportunity.

Claude's independent assessment

Claude completed a separate review in the desktop app after the CLI failed with an expired OAuth session. Open the Claude conversation. This was a bounded public-web review; Claude reported ten searches/page fetches and no original Facebook policy comments read.

  • Verdict: refine. Timely subject; reject the original slogan for this campaign. “Control” can read as a workaround, and “narrative” can imply influencing reviews.
  • Audience: independent hosts with one to five properties, personal hosts and hosts replacing printed binders. Hosts seeking a way to enforce cleaning chores are a poor fit.
  • Stronger hooks: “Helpful, not homework” and “Meet your host. Find the coffee. Know trash day.” Claude found “Keep your welcome personal” accurate but too generic to carry the policy moment by itself.
  • Avoid the hostel/hotel comparison: in this debate, guests may hear it as justification for unpaid cleaning. Keep the idea of a home with character, and demonstrate hospitality.
  • Test purchase intent: qualified demo visits, setup and actual guest use matter more than angry comments or likes.

Our editorial refinement: Claude also proposed “Rules live in your listing. Your welcome lives here.” That is useful educational copy, but too procedural for every sales post. Lead the commercial creative with the personal welcome and concrete utility. Use the rules distinction in supporting educational content.

Claude suggested a published-rules reference block and prompts for obligation language. These are product ideas, not verified implemented features, and are not included in the campaign claims. Its copy saying hosts approve everything was also tightened: current source supports screening, review of flagged content and host moderation, rather than mandatory manual approval of every post. Its proposed small-sample A/B threshold is only exploratory; it cannot establish a statistically reliable winner.

Five campaign drafts

Each uses an existing product reason: useful answers, personal hospitality, guest memories, host moderation or private feedback. Visuals below are briefs, not finished assets.

Facebook · personal host photo or candid property-preparation scene · reason 2

Hosting expectations are changing. A personal welcome still has a place.

Introduce the people behind the property. Share what makes it special, how things work, and the local spots you love. DigiStayBook brings your welcome and practical house information together with a guestbook guests can open from a QR display.

Your welcome. Your words. The details guests need.

2. Know trash day. Enjoy your stay.

Facebook carousel / Pinterest checklist · clear practical detail · reasons 1 and 10

The useful details are often the little ones: where the recycling goes, which drawer has spare bags, and when pickup happens.

Example to adapt to your property: “The indoor trash and recycling containers are under the sink. Pickup is Tuesday morning. We handle taking the outdoor cans to the curb.”

A welcome can explain how the home works without turning into a chore list. Keep your information accurate and consistent with the terms guests saw before booking.

Explore an example: https://digistaybook.com/stay/demo-cottage?utm_source=facebook&utm_medium=organic_social&utm_campaign=host_voice_oct30&utm_content=trash_day

Visual brief: genuine property photo plus a clearly labeled illustrative note. Use only when the host really handles the cans. A Pinterest version becomes a saveable “five details for your guest welcome” checklist with a separately tagged Pinterest destination; avoid repeating the feed layout.

3. Meet the person behind the place.

Facebook photo essay / short video · human hosts · reasons 2 and 3

How to test it

Use the period before November 20 as an educational opportunity, then keep the strongest useful-welcome creative after the policy news fades. The current merged calendar already contains personal-welcome and practical-information posts: test selected replacements after item approval, rather than starting a competing daily calendar. Preserve approval history and reconcile anything already scheduled before changing it.

  1. Compare the concrete “Your welcome. Your words.” concept with “Helpful, not homework.” Keep the destination and call to action comparable; tag each creative separately. Suggested cadence: two approved Facebook posts across a week, one practical Pinterest checklist and supporting Stories. Choose actual dates against the existing calendar. If posting times are added, use US Eastern with Sydney beside them.
  2. With later outreach approval, talk to 8–10 current hosts and show the draft to five travelers. Ask what they believe the product does, what they would put in it, whether they would use it, and whether any wording implies compulsory chores. Do not turn research participants into endorsements.
  3. Record outbound clicks, tagged demo sessions, verified sign-ups and first-property setup in the existing scorecard. Distinguish scan/use evidence from host preview traffic. Use host-confirmed QR placement and guest use in a small pilot if analytics cannot distinguish them. Missing data stays blank, not zero.
  4. Judge fit by activated properties and useful host conversations. Small samples support learning, not a statistically reliable conversion claim. Stop or rewrite if people mainly ask how to bypass rules, if travelers read it as a hidden chore list, or if attention produces no setup interest.

The tracked demo links above use the existing route; no new short-link redirects or app deployment are needed for this draft. They are proposed campaign tracking, not evidence that conversion events or scan-per-stay reporting already exist. All posting, scheduling, spending and outreach remain unperformed.

Language and product boundaries

Use no booking-platform names or logos in consumer-facing campaign copy. Recognizable context can come from “hosting expectations are changing,” without suggesting endorsement or making allegations. Omitting a company name does not guarantee freedom from legal claims; these drafts have not received legal clearance.

Do not promise better guests, less damage, fewer messages, better reviews or more revenue. Do not pitch the guide as a policy exemption. Do not use “treat it like a hostel,” “guests are treating homes badly now,” or “put the rules they banned in your guestbook.” Keep essential safety and legally required physical notices available; this campaign does not claim a QR guide replaces them.
