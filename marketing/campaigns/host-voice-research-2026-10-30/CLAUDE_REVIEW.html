What is still a marketing hypothesis

  • That hosts in this cycle want a guest welcome product, rather than a compliance fix or a way to vent.

  • That a QR guide changes guest behavior, messages, damage or reviews. You correctly note there is no evidence for this, and no campaign copy should imply it.

  • That “humanizing” the host is a purchase driver rather than a nice-to-have.

Why “Control your own narrative” fails

  • It reads as a workaround. Airbnb has just said rules belong only in the listing, not on signs or in messages. A slogan about “control,” attached to an in-home QR placard, invites hosts to read the product as the place to put rules Airbnb won’t accept. That attracts the wrong buyers and creates platform risk for them.

  • “Narrative” suggests influencing reviews. That clashes with your own boundary of not implying control over third-party reviews.

  • It aligns the brand with the host backlash. That backlash is aimed at the guests who will scan your placard. Your product is guest-facing, so a host-grievance frame poisons the guest experience it’s meant to improve.

The opportunity that does hold up

Every host has to review their listing before November 20. The clean message is:

rules go in the listing; everything else that makes a stay easy can go in your welcome.

That is timely, accurate and non-adversarial.

2. Positioning, audience, objections and slogans

Positioning

Draw a clear line:

“The listing holds the rules. Your welcome holds everything else.”

That message comes with a product requirement. The guide should mirror the listing’s rules and requests, not add to them. Consider:

  • A “From your listing” block where hosts paste their published rules verbatim.

  • A soft prompt when a host types obligation language (“must,” “required,” “fee,” “fine”) outside that block.

This turns the policy into a feature and gives you a credible answer to the “isn’t this just signs?” objection.

Audience

Independent hosts with 1–5 homes who used to keep printed binders or house manuals

Primary

They already value a welcome and have content to migrate. The policy forces them to sort rules from information.

Hosts who visit or live nearby and personally care about hospitality

Strong

The human-host and local-favorites features actually differentiate here.

Co-hosts and small managers who answer repetitive “where is…?” messages

Test

Any benefit is unproven, so offer it as something to try, not a promised saving.

Hosts angry that they can no longer require dishes or bin runs

Poor

They want enforcement, which the product cannot legitimately provide. Expect high churn and policy-adjacent misuse.

Objections to prepare for

  • “Isn’t this just signs by another name?” No. It’s information and hospitality; rules stay in the listing. Show the mirror block.

  • “Will this get my listing flagged?” You can’t promise it won’t. Say the product is designed to keep rules where Airbnb requires them, and leave compliance responsibility with the host.

  • “Guests won’t scan a QR code.” That’s plausible, and a reason to measure scans per stay in the pilot.

  • “I already have a guidebook.” Lead with what yours adds: host introduction, guest notes and photos with moderation, and private feedback.

  • Guest privacy in photos and notes. Moderation and consent defaults need to be visible to the guest.

  • Private feedback. Present it as a way for guests to flag issues, never as a filter that steers happy guests to public reviews and others away. Also check the platform’s review policy before saying anything about reviews.

  • “No-name” copy. Leaving out the competitor’s name reduces confusion but gives no legal protection, as you noted. Avoid logos, colors and lookalike language too.

Slogans

“Control your own narrative”

Drop. Sounds like a workaround and implies influence over reviews.

“Keep your welcome personal” (your alternative)

Safe and accurate but generic. It has no link to the policy moment, so it won’t ride the news cycle. Fine as an evergreen tagline.

“Your home has a story. Let guests feel part of it.” (your alternative)

Warm, but “feel part of it” can read to guests as being asked to contribute or participate, and it says nothing about what’s practically useful. It also leans on sentiment while your best proof is utility. Keep it for brand pages, not the launch.

“Rules live in your listing. Your welcome lives here.”

Recommended for the policy moment. Accurate, clear on compliance, and repels the wrong buyers.

“Helpful, not homework.”

Strong for guest-facing creative. Signals there are no hidden chores.

“Answers before they have to ask.”

Good for host utility. Avoid any implied claim like “fewer messages.”

“Meet your host. Find the coffee. Know trash day.”

Concrete, human and practical. Works as the visual hook.

On the hostel-versus-hotel comparison: I’d keep it out of public copy. In this news cycle any “we’re not a hotel” framing will be heard as “so you clean.” A secondary source noted one host’s worry that the policy leans toward a hotel-like model. That is exactly the debate to avoid entering.

5. Five campaign concepts (US English, ready for review)

None of these names a competitor or includes prices, outcome guarantees or customer quotes.

  1. “The line” (host-facing, policy moment)

Rules belong in your listing. Everything else — the Wi-Fi password, how the coffee maker works, the taco place you’d send your best friend to — belongs in a welcome that sounds like you. DigiStayBook is a browser guide your guests open from a QR card. No app, no account. Your rules stay where guests saw them before booking; your hospitality gets a home of its own.

Visual:

A split card. Left: “Your listing: the rules.” Right: “Your welcome: everything else.”

  1. “Trash day, minus the guesswork” (guest perspective, practical)

You just arrived. Where does the recycling go? Is tomorrow pickup day — and do you need to do anything? A good welcome answers that in five seconds:

“Kitchen bins are under the sink: blue for recycling, gray for everything else. Pickup is Tuesday morning, and we handle the outdoor bins — nothing for you to move. If the kitchen bin fills up, spare bags are in the drawer.”

That’s what DigiStayBook is for: clear, friendly answers so guests can get back to their vacation.

Compliance note:

The example says plainly that the host handles the outdoor bins. Don’t publish a version where “pickup is Tuesday” sits next to a bin location with no clarification, because it reads as an implied task.

  1. “Meet the person behind the place” (human hosts)

Somebody chose the reading lamp by the window. Somebody knows which beach is calmer in the morning. A short hello from the people behind the home turns a rental into a place someone cares about — and gives guests a reason to feel welcome. Add your story, your favorite spots, and a photo of you. Guests scan once and they’re in.

  1. “Helpful, not homework” (guest-facing reassurance)

A welcome guide should feel like a friend’s note on the counter, not a to-do list. DigiStayBook keeps it to the good stuff: how things work, where to eat, and who’s on the other end if something comes up. Your stay, your pace.

Use:

Placard back side, social graphic, and landing-page hero for the guest view.

  1. “Leave a note for the next guest” (guestbook)

The best part of a paper guestbook was reading who came before you. DigiStayBook brings that back: guests can leave a note or a photo, hosts approve what’s shared, and anything private goes straight to the host instead of the wall. A little history for every home.

Compliance note:

Never describe private feedback as a way to keep criticism out of public reviews.

6. Small organic validation plan (no spending, no publishing by me)

Goal:

find out whether the “rules in your listing, welcome here” message brings in

qualified

hosts who actually activate, not just reactions.

Before November 20 (while hosts are editing listings)

  • Two landing-page variants, each with a demo guide: A “The line” (Concept 1) and B “Meet your host” (Concept 3). Use separate UTM links per channel and variant.

  • Organic channels, used only where the rules allow: your own email list or social accounts, groups that permit vendor posts on designated days, and 1:1 outreach to hosts you know. Disclose that you’re the owners every time.

  • Interviews with 8–10 hosts. Ask:• “What did you move out of signs or messages this week?”• “What’s left that guests still need to know?”• Then show them the guide.

  • Guest read-test with 5 people unconnected to you. Have them read the Concept 2 trash text and the sample guide, and ask: “Is anything here asking you to do a chore?” Any “yes” means a rewrite.

What to measure

Qualified visit

Visits from self-identified current hosts

A one-click “I host on a platform / I’m exploring” choice, plus at least 30 seconds on the demo guide

Interest

Demo or sign-up requests per qualified visit

Form completed

Activation

Share of sign-ups that publish a guide and print or place a placard within 14 days

Guide published, with placard placement confirmed

Real use

Guest scans per stay in the first 30 days

Scans divided by stays the host reports

Fit check

Share of published guides that add rules not in the listing

Manual review of content in the “rules mirror” block versus elsewhere. If this is high, the message is attracting the wrong use.

Engagement counts as a secondary measure only.

Likes and comments on policy posts are driven by anger and predict little.

Deciding on results.

Set thresholds before you start, for example “variant A wins if its qualified-visit-to-activation rate is clearly higher at 20 or more qualified visits per variant.” Treat any result as directional because the samples will be small. Report the raw counts, not just percentages.

Stop signals

  • Interviewees mainly want a way to enforce chores.

  • The guest read-test flags hidden tasks.

  • Activated guides routinely contain new rules.

Any of these means repositioning before scaling.
